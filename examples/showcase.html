The delta is not a function, it is a distribution, so what it does matters more than what it is, and calling it a spike of infinite height is only a picture. Math sits inside styles too: the pulse of width w has area 1, and the Dirac delta article has the history. Why does the transform of a unit pulse tend to one? Because the exponential is close to one while the pulse lasts.

Notation used below

The sifting property

The delta is defined by what it does under an integral. For any smooth f(t),

∫−∞∞f(t)δ(t)dt=f(0)(1)

and shifting the spike to a picks out f(a) instead. The next section turns this into a transform, and (3) is the one-line consequence. The sketch below is from ./figure.png, a placeholder path that does not exist in the repository.

One way to see the delta is as the limit of rectangular pulses of unit area:

pw(t)={1w0≤t≤w0otherwise

Each pulse has area one, because its height is the reciprocal of its width:

∫0wpw(t)dt=∫0w1wdt=1

and the delta is what is left when the width is sent to zero from above:

δ(t)=limw→0+pw(t)

The pulse lives on the interval t∈[0,w], and its transform is easy to write down:

ℒ{pw(t)}(s)=1−e−wsws

When ws is small this is close to one, which is the transform of the delta: 1−e−wsws≈1.

A narrow pulse needs a wide spectrum. With energy and angular frequency tied by E=ℏω, the pulse duration and the spread of energies obey

ΔEΔt≥ℏ2

and the same rule in plain signal terms is ΔtΔω≥12. The period of a wave is T=2πω, typed here with the characters π and ω instead of the names pi and omega. They look the same, but they are different variables to the tool.

Unit step: H(t)

The antiderivative of the delta is the unit step:

H(t)={1t≥00t<0

Written as a derivative, it says ddtH(t)=δ(t), or in Leibniz form dHdt=δ(t), or with a prime H′=δ. The step is not continuous at the origin, so the one-sided limit matters: limt→0+H(t)=1.

Remark. The step needs a convention at zero.

  • Some books put H(0)=0.
  • Others put H(0)=12.

The transform does not care, because a single point has no integral.

def H(t):
    return 1.0 if t >= 0 else 0.0

The Laplace transform

The transform sends a signal to a function of a complex frequency s:

ℒ{f}(s)=∫0−∞f(t)e−stdt(2)

The lower limit is 0− so that a delta sitting at the origin is counted fully. With that, the sifting property gives the first entry of the table directly:

ℒ{δ(t)}(s)=∫0−∞δ(t)e−stdt=e−st|t=0=1(3)

The transform of the delta has no poles at all, Pol(1)=∅. The transform itself is a map, ℒ:ℰ→ℱ, and a signal goes to its transform by f↦ℒ{f}(s).

f(t)F(s)δ(t)1H(t)1stnn!sn+1e−at1s+asin(ωt)ωs2+ω2cos(ωt)ss2+ω2

Three properties do most of the work:

  1. Linearity, which the macro linear states: ℒ{f+g}(s)=ℒ{f}(s)+ℒ{g}(s).
  2. Differentiation in time.
    • First order: ℒ{f′}(s)=sℒ{f}(s)−f(0).
    • Second order: ℒ{f′′}(s)=s2ℒ{f}(s)−sf(0)−f′(0).
  3. Reality: for real f, F(s¯)=F(s)¯.

The bar is the complex conjugate, so the transform of a real signal takes conjugate values at conjugate points. For the derivatives of a product, such as a smooth function times a delta, the Leibniz rule is the tool. The comments inside this form explain its parts, and none of them reach the page:

dndtn(fg)=∑k=0n(nk)(dkdtkf)dn−kdtn−kg

Integrating by parts once puts the derivative of the delta onto the test function:

∫−∞∞f(t)ddtδ(t)dt=−f′(0)(4)

Each time a decaying exponential is differentiated it brings down a factor of −a, so the second derivative is d2dt2e−at=(−a)2e−at. The parentheses around the −a were not written in the source: the renderer adds them to a negative base. The decay rate gives a time constant, τ=1a.

Pulse trains

A train of deltas spaced by T transforms to a geometric series:

ℒ{∑k=0∞δ(t−kT)}(s)=∑k=0∞e−kTs=11−e−Ts(5)

Its first three pulses are u3=δ(t)+δ(t−T)+δ(t−2×T). The number of pulses that have arrived by time t is ⌊tT⌋+1, and a sampler that has to resolve a dynamic range AmaxAmin needs ⌈log2(AmaxAmin)⌉ bits.

A chain of n first-order filters multiplies the factors:

H(s)=∏j=1n11+sτj

If the stages are identical and the whole chain has a gain of Gtotal at zero frequency, each stage has the gain Gtotaln.

The damped oscillator

A mass on a spring with damping, hit by an impulse of size J, obeys

mx¨+cx˙+kx=Jδ(t)(6)

With zero initial conditions, (2) turns each derivative into a power of s, and with m=1 from here on the equation becomes algebra:

(s2+cs+k)X(s)=J

The sum in front of X(s) is wrapped in parentheses, and again they were not written: a sum under a product gets them. The roots of that sum are the poles. They depend on

D=(c2)2−k

where the renderer put parentheses around the fraction because it is raised to a power. One pole is at

s=−c2+D

and the other has the opposite sign in front of the root. Together they form a set, Pol(H)={p1,p2}. The sign of D decides what happens, and exactly one of the cases holds:

¬D<0⟺D>0∨D=0

The poles are real exactly when D is not negative:

Pol(H)⊆ℝ⟺¬D<0

Stability of the damped system, with a plain-words side, comes down to the signs of the coefficients:

the system is stable⟺c>0∧k>0

The same system in first-order form uses a state vector and a matrix. Here the state is the pair of position and velocity:

ddt(x1x2)=(01−k−c)(x1x2)

The matrix whose determinant matters is s times the identity minus that one:

(s−1ks+c)=s(1001)−(01−k−c)

and its determinant is the same polynomial as before:

|s−1ks+c|=s2+cs+k

For D<0 the poles are complex, with imaginary part ωd=k−c24. A first-order lag with time constant τ has the frequency response 11+iωτ, whose phase lag can be written with an arcsine:

φ=−sin−1(ωτ1+(ωτ)2)

The same angle is more often given by its tangent, tan(φ)=−ωτ, or as an arctangent, φ=−tan−1(ωτ). The envelope of the oscillation shrinks to a fraction ε of its start after

tsettle=2ln(1ε)c

The result: for D<0 and zero start, the displacement after the hammer blow is the impulse response, boxed because it is what the note was for:

h(t)=1ωde−ct2sin(ωdt)(7)

Equation (6) came first and (7) answers it. The Fourier version sets s=iω and reads f^(ω)=ℒ{f}(iω). The same transform in space and time, for a transport equation with a source that fires once at t=0:

∂∂tu+c∂∂xu=δ(t)g(x)

and transformed in time it becomes an ordinary equation in x:

sU(x,s)+c∂∂xU=g(x)

In three dimensions, with the position as a vector, the sifting property integrates over all of space:

∫ℝ3f(r→)δ(r→)dr→=f(0)

Aside: signs and rules

The transform is only defined for signals that do not grow faster than an exponential. Let ℰ be that class:

f∈ℰ⟺(∃M.∃a.∀t.t≥0⟹|f(t)|≤Meat)

The region of convergence of a sum contains the overlap of the two regions, and the poles of a product are among the poles of its factors:

ROC(f+g)⊇ROC(f)∩ROC(g)
Pol(FG)⊆Pol(F)∪Pol(G)

Stability of the damped system is a statement with a quantifier and a hypothesis: if both coefficients are positive, every pole has a negative real part.

c>0∧k>0⟹(∀p.p∈Pol(H)⟹Re(p)<0)

When the spring is present the origin is never a pole: k≠0⟹0∉Pol(H), because at the origin the denominator is just k.

f∈ℰg∈ℰs∈ℂℒ{f+g}(s)=ℒ{f}(s)+ℒ{g}(s)T-Lin

Fixing a gain with let

let binds a variable for the rest of the note, and every use after it is replaced by the value, so I keep it for the end and use names that appear nowhere else. A gain of 4 in front of a first-order lag:

G(s)=4s+2

The next binding captures the value of gain as it is now, so boost holds 2 times 4, not the name gain. A variable is defined only once per note, so a different gain gets a name of its own:

boost=2×4
retuned=10

The quoted name stays a name and the unquoted one shows its value.

Evaluation, broken forms and tooling

No evaluator ships yet, so a live form headed by eval would render an error box. It is written like this and has to stand alone:

(eval (+ 1 2))

A form with a mistake in it, such as a keyword with the wrong number of arguments, becomes a red error box in place of that one form, with the message inside it, and the rest of the note renders as usual. The command line prints the same message with its position. This is what a broken form looks like, shown only as source:

(show (frac 1))
